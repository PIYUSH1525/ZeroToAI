  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-blue-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      1. What is a Derivative? (The Sensitivity Dial)
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        Forget memorizing dusty textbook tables for a moment. In Artificial Intelligence, a <strong className="text-white">Derivative</strong> answers one simple, practical question: <em className="text-blue-300">&quot;If I nudge this input knob $x$ by a tiny amount, how much does the output $f(x)$ change?&quot;</em>
      </div>
      <div>
        Geometrically, the derivative of a function $f(x)$ at any point is the <strong className="text-white">instantaneous slope of the tangent line</strong> touching the curve at that exact point:
      </div>
      <div className="my-6 rounded-2xl border border-blue-400/20 bg-blue-400/[0.04] px-6 py-8 text-center text-lg">
        <div>$$f^\prime(x) = \dfrac{df}{dx} = \lim_{h \to 0} \dfrac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$$</div>
      </div>
      <div className="grid gap-4 md:grid-cols-3">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/25 bg-emerald-500/[0.05] p-5 text-center">
          <div className="mb-2 text-lg font-bold text-emerald-300">Positive Slope ($\dfrac{df}{dx} \gt 0$)</div>
          <div className="mb-2 font-mono text-xs text-slate-400">Curve goes UPHILL</div>
          <div className="text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            Increasing $x$ increases the output $f(x)$. To <strong className="text-white">reduce</strong> error, we must <strong className="text-emerald-300">decrease $x$</strong> (step left).
          </div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-rose-500/25 bg-rose-500/[0.05] p-5 text-center">
          <div className="mb-2 text-lg font-bold text-rose-300">Negative Slope ($\dfrac{df}{dx} \lt 0$)</div>
          <div className="mb-2 font-mono text-xs text-slate-400">Curve goes DOWNHILL</div>
          <div className="text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            Increasing $x$ decreases the output $f(x)$. To <strong className="text-white">reduce</strong> error, we must <strong className="text-rose-300">increase $x$</strong> (step right).
          </div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-cyan-500/25 bg-cyan-500/[0.05] p-5 text-center">
          <div className="mb-2 text-lg font-bold text-cyan-300">Zero Slope ($\dfrac{df}{dx} = 0$)</div>
          <div className="mb-2 font-mono text-xs text-slate-400">Flat Valley / Peak</div>
          <div className="text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            The curve is completely flat. We have reached a <strong className="text-white">minimum</strong>, maximum, or saddle point!
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-2xl border border-blue-400/15 bg-blue-400/[0.05] p-6">
        <h3 className="mb-4 text-lg font-bold text-white">Intuitive Numerical Example ($f(x) = x^2$):</h3>
        <div className="space-y-4 text-sm">
          <div>Suppose our error function is $f(x) = x^2$ and our current weight is $x = 3$, giving an error of $f(3) = 9$. What happens if we nudge $x$ up by a tiny step $h = 0.001$ to $x = 3.001$?</div>
          <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-slate-950/70 px-6 py-6 text-center text-base">
            <div>$$\dfrac{f(3.001) - f(3)}{0.001} = \dfrac{9.006001 - 9}{0.001} = 6.001 \approx 6$$</div>
          </div>
          <div>At $x = 3$, the output grows <strong className="text-white">$6$ times faster</strong> than our tiny nudge! That agrees with the calculus rule $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2) = 2x = 2(3) = 6$.</div>
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-violet-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      2. Essential Derivative Rules Used in Machine Learning
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        You only need a small toolkit of basic derivative rules to understand every loss function and activation function in Deep Learning:
      </div>
      <div className="my-6 overflow-x-auto rounded-2xl border border-white/10">
        <table className="w-full min-w-[720px] border-collapse text-left text-sm">
          <thead className="bg-white/[0.06] text-white">
            <tr>
              <th className="border-b border-white/10 px-5 py-4 font-semibold">Rule Name</th>
              <th className="border-b border-white/10 px-5 py-4 font-semibold">Function $f(x)$</th>
              <th className="border-b border-white/10 px-5 py-4 font-semibold">Derivative $f^\prime(x)$</th>
              <th className="border-b border-white/10 px-5 py-4 font-semibold">Where It Appears in AI</th>
            </tr>
          </thead>
          <tbody className="text-slate-300">
            <tr className="border-b border-white/10">
              <td className="px-5 py-4 font-semibold text-white">Constant Rule</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">$f(x) = c$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4 text-cyan-300">$0$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">Fixed labels $y$ or unlearnable constants have zero gradient.</td>
            </tr>
            <tr className="border-b border-white/10">
              <td className="px-5 py-4 font-semibold text-white">Linear Rule</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">$f(x) = wx + b$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4 text-cyan-300">$w$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">Dense neural layers and Linear Regression slopes.</td>
            </tr>
            <tr className="border-b border-white/10">
              <td className="px-5 py-4 font-semibold text-white">Power Rule</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">$f(x) = x^n$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4 text-cyan-300">$n x^{n-1}$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">Mean Squared Error (MSE): derivative of $e^2$ is $2e$.</td>
            </tr>
            <tr className="border-b border-white/10">
              <td className="px-5 py-4 font-semibold text-white">Exponential Rule</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">$f(x) = e^x$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4 text-cyan-300">$e^x$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">Powers Softmax, Sigmoid, and Tanh activations.</td>
            </tr>
            <tr>
              <td className="px-5 py-4 font-semibold text-white">Natural Log Rule</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">$f(x) = \ln(x)$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4 text-cyan-300">$\dfrac{1}{x}$</td>
              <td className="px-5 py-4">Cross-Entropy Loss ($\mathcal{L} = -\ln(\hat{y})$) in classifiers &amp; LLMs.</td>
            </tr>
          </tbody>
        </table>
      </div>
      <h3 className="mt-8 text-xl font-bold text-white">Derivatives of Neural Network Activation Functions</h3>
      <div className="grid gap-5 md:grid-cols-2">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-violet-500/25 bg-violet-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-2 font-bold text-violet-300">1. Sigmoid Activation ($\sigma(z)$)</div>
          <div className="mb-4 text-sm text-slate-300">
            Squashes any real number into $(0, 1)$. Its derivative has a remarkably clean self-referential form:
          </div>
          <div className="rounded-lg bg-slate-950 px-4 py-5 text-center text-sm">
            <div>$$\sigma(z) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$$</div>
            <div className="mt-3">$$\sigma^\prime(z) = \sigma(z)\big(1 - \sigma(z)\big)$$</div>
          </div>
          <div className="mt-3 text-xs text-slate-400">
            Notice: Its maximum slope is only $0.25$ (at $z=0$). Multiplying many $0.25$s across deep layers causes the <strong className="text-rose-400">Vanishing Gradient Problem</strong>!
          </div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/25 bg-emerald-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-2 font-bold text-emerald-300">2. ReLU Activation ($\max(0, z)$)</div>
          <div className="mb-4 text-sm text-slate-300">
            The modern default in Deep Learning. Its derivative is simply a binary switch ($1$ if positive, $0$ if negative):
          </div>
          <div className="rounded-lg bg-slate-950 px-4 py-5 text-center text-sm">
            <div>$$\text{ReLU}(z) = \max(0, z)$$</div>
            <div className="mt-3">$$\text{ReLU}^\prime(z) = 1 \text{ (for } z \gt 0\text{)} \quad \text{or} \quad 0 \text{ (for } z \lt 0\text{)}$$</div>
          </div>
          <div className="mt-3 text-xs text-slate-400">
            Because the slope is a constant $1$ for all positive inputs, gradients flow backward through deep networks without shrinking!
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="mt-8 rounded-2xl border border-amber-500/25 bg-[#0A0D16] p-6 shadow-[0_0_25px_rgba(245,158,11,0.05)]">
        <div className="mb-2 flex items-center gap-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-widest text-amber-400">
          <span>⚡ Knowledge Check </span>
        </div>
        <div className="mb-5 text-base font-semibold text-white">
          You are training a single weight $w$. At its current value $w = 5$, you compute the derivative of the Loss with respect to $w$ and find $\dfrac{dL}{dw} = -8$. To DECREASE the Loss, how should you adjust $w$?
        </div>
        <div className="grid gap-4 md:grid-cols-2">
          <details className="group">
            <summary className="flex cursor-pointer items-center justify-between rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-slate-200 transition-colors hover:bg-white/[0.06] marker:content-['']">
              <span>A) Increase w (move right, e.g. to 5.1)</span>
              <span className="text-amber-400 transition group-open:rotate-180">▼</span>
            </summary>
            <div className="mt-3 rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/30 bg-emerald-500/10 p-4 text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
              <strong className="mb-1 block text-emerald-400">✓ Correct!</strong>
              <span>A negative derivative ($\dfrac{dL}{dw} = -8$) means the error curve slopes downhill as $w$ increases. Mathematically, $w_{\text{new}} = w - \alpha(-8) = w + 8\alpha$, which increases $w$ to reduce the Loss.</span>
            </div>
          </details>
          <details className="group">
            <summary className="flex cursor-pointer items-center justify-between rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-slate-200 transition-colors hover:bg-white/[0.06] marker:content-['']">
              <span>B) Decrease w (move left, e.g. to 4.9)</span>
              <span className="text-amber-400 transition group-open:rotate-180">▼</span>
            </summary>
            <div className="mt-3 rounded-xl border border-rose-500/30 bg-rose-500/10 p-4 text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
              <strong className="mb-1 block text-rose-400">✕ Incorrect.</strong>
              <span>Because the slope is negative ($-8$), moving left (decreasing $w$) walks uphill on the error curve and makes the Loss worse.</span>
            </div>
          </details>
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-emerald-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      3. Partial Derivatives: Isolating One Knob Among Millions
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        Real AI models never depend on just one variable $x$. Even a simple Linear Regression model $\hat{y} = wx + b$ has two parameters ($w$ and $b$), and modern LLMs have billions. How do we measure the slope when there are multiple variables?
      </div>
      <div>
        We use a <strong className="text-white">Partial Derivative</strong>, written with the curly symbol $\partial$ instead of $d$. To take the partial derivative $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w}$, you <strong className="text-emerald-300">pretend every other variable is a frozen constant number</strong> and differentiate normally with respect to $w$ alone!
      </div>
      <div className="my-6 rounded-2xl border border-emerald-400/20 bg-emerald-400/[0.04] px-6 py-8 text-center">
        <div>$$f(w, b) = w^2 b + 3b^2$$</div>
        <div className="mt-6 grid gap-4 text-sm sm:grid-cols-2">
          <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-slate-950 p-5">
            <div className="mb-3 font-bold text-cyan-300">Partial w.r.t. $w$ (Freeze $b$ as constant)</div>
            <div>$$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial w} = 2wb + 0 = 2wb$$</div>
          </div>
          <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-slate-950 p-5">
            <div className="mb-3 font-bold text-violet-300">Partial w.r.t. $b$ (Freeze $w$ as constant)</div>
            <div>$$\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial b} = w^2(1) + 6b = w^2 + 6b$$</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-5">
        <strong className="text-white">Geometric Intuition (3D Mountain Surface):</strong> Imagine standing on a 3D mountain where East-West is $w$, North-South is $b$, and altitude is Loss $L(w, b)$.
        <div className="mt-3 space-y-2 text-sm text-slate-300">
          <div>• $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ is the slope of the mountain if you walk strictly <strong className="text-cyan-300">East-West</strong> (keeping North-South fixed).</div>
          <div>• $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b}$ is the slope of the mountain if you walk strictly <strong className="text-violet-300">North-South</strong> (keeping East-West fixed).</div>
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-orange-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      4. The Gradient Vector ($\nabla L$): Packing All Partial Derivatives Together
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        Instead of handling thousands of partial derivatives separately, we pack every parameter&apos;s partial derivative into a single column vector called the <strong className="text-orange-400">Gradient Vector</strong>, denoted by the nabla symbol $\nabla L$:
      </div>
      <div className="my-6 rounded-2xl border border-orange-400/20 bg-orange-400/[0.04] px-6 py-8 text-center text-lg">
        <div>$$\nabla L(\mathbf{w}) = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} \\[10pt] \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_2} \\[6pt] \vdots \\[6pt] \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_n} \end{bmatrix}$$</div>
      </div>
      <div className="my-6 rounded-2xl border border-orange-500/25 bg-slate-950/80 p-6 text-center">
        <div className="mb-2 text-xs font-semibold uppercase tracking-[0.2em] text-orange-400">
          The Golden Rule of the Gradient Vector
        </div>
        <div className="text-base font-semibold text-white">
          In high-dimensional space, the Gradient Vector $\nabla L$ ALWAYS points in the direction of STEEPEST ASCENT (maximum error increase). Therefore, $-\nabla L$ ALWAYS points in the direction of STEEPEST DESCENT (fastest error reduction).
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-2xl border border-blue-400/15 bg-blue-400/[0.05] p-6">
        <h3 className="mb-4 text-lg font-bold text-white">Worked Example: Deriving Linear Regression Gradients From Scratch</h3>
        <div className="space-y-4 text-sm">
          <div>Consider a single training sample $(x, y)$ with prediction $\hat{y} = wx + b$ and Squared Error Loss $L(w, b) = (wx + b - y)^2$. Let $e = (wx + b - y)$ be our prediction error:</div>
          <div className="grid gap-4 md:grid-cols-2">
            <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-slate-950/80 p-5 text-center">
              <div className="mb-3 text-xs font-bold uppercase text-cyan-300">Partial Derivative w.r.t. Weight $w$</div>
              <div>$$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 2(wx + b - y) \cdot x = 2e \cdot x$$</div>
            </div>
            <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-slate-950/80 p-5 text-center">
              <div className="mb-3 text-xs font-bold uppercase text-violet-300">Partial Derivative w.r.t. Bias $b$</div>
              <div>$$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial b} = 2(wx + b - y) \cdot 1 = 2e$$</div>
            </div>
          </div>
          <div>
            Look at how intuitive that math is: the weight gradient $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w} = 2ex$ is simply <strong className="text-white">Error $\times$ Input</strong>! If input $x = 0$, that weight did not contribute to the mistake, so its gradient is $0$.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="mt-8 rounded-2xl border border-amber-500/25 bg-[#0A0D16] p-6 shadow-[0_0_25px_rgba(245,158,11,0.05)]">
        <div className="mb-2 flex items-center gap-2 text-xs font-bold uppercase tracking-widest text-amber-400">
          <span>⚡ Knowledge Check </span>
        </div>
        <div className="mb-5 text-base font-semibold text-white">
          Given the two-variable function $L(w_1, w_2) = 3w_1^2 + 4w_1 w_2 + 5w_2^2$, what is the partial derivative $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_1}$ evaluated at the point $(w_1 = 1, w_2 = 2)$?
        </div>
        <div className="grid gap-4 md:grid-cols-2">
          <details className="group">
            <summary className="flex cursor-pointer items-center justify-between rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-slate-200 transition-colors hover:bg-white/[0.06] marker:content-['']">
              <span>A) 14</span>
              <span className="text-amber-400 transition group-open:rotate-180">▼</span>
            </summary>
            <div className="mt-3 rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/30 bg-emerald-500/10 p-4 text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
              <strong className="mb-1 block text-emerald-400">✓ Correct!</strong>
              <span>Treating $w_2$ as a constant, $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_1} = 6w_1 + 4w_2 + 0$. Plugging in $w_1 = 1$ and $w_2 = 2$ gives $6(1) + 4(2) = 6 + 8 = 14$.</span>
            </div>
          </details>
          <details className="group">
            <summary className="flex cursor-pointer items-center justify-between rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] px-4 py-3 text-sm font-semibold text-slate-200 transition-colors hover:bg-white/[0.06] marker:content-['']">
              <span>B) 34</span>
              <span className="text-amber-400 transition group-open:rotate-180">▼</span>
            </summary>
            <div className="mt-3 rounded-xl border border-rose-500/30 bg-rose-500/10 p-4 text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
              <strong className="mb-1 block text-rose-400">✕ Incorrect.</strong>
              <span>When differentiating with respect to $w_1$, the term $5w_2^2$ has no $w_1$ in it, so it acts like a constant and its derivative is $0$ (not $10w_2$).</span>
            </div>
          </details>
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-cyan-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      5. The Chain Rule: The Engine Behind Backpropagation
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        A neural network is a <strong className="text-white">nested chain of functions</strong>: the weights $w$ compute a linear sum $z = wx + b$, which feeds into an activation $a = \sigma(z)$, which feeds into the Loss $L(a)$. How do we find $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w}$ when $w$ is buried three layers deep?
      </div>
      <div>
        We use the <strong className="text-cyan-300">Chain Rule of Calculus</strong>, which states that the derivative of a composite chain of functions is simply the <strong className="text-white">product of the local derivatives along the path</strong>:
      </div>
      <div className="my-6 rounded-2xl border border-cyan-400/20 bg-cyan-400/[0.04] px-6 py-8 text-center text-lg">
        <div>$$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a} \cdot \dfrac{\partial a}{\partial z} \cdot \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial w}$$</div>
      </div>
      <div className="grid gap-4 md:grid-cols-3">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-5 text-center">
          <div className="mb-2 font-mono text-sm font-bold text-rose-300">$\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a}$</div>
          <div className="text-xs text-slate-300">How much the <strong className="text-white">Loss $L$</strong> changes when the <strong className="text-white">Activation $a$</strong> changes.</div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-5 text-center">
          <div className="mb-2 font-mono text-sm font-bold text-violet-300">$\dfrac{\partial a}{\partial z}$</div>
          <div className="text-xs text-slate-300">How much the <strong className="text-white">Activation $a$</strong> changes when the <strong className="text-white">Logit $z$</strong> changes ($\sigma^\prime(z)$).</div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-5 text-center">
          <div className="mb-2 font-mono text-sm font-bold text-cyan-300">$\dfrac{\partial z}{\partial w}$</div>
          <div className="text-xs text-slate-300">How much the <strong className="text-white">Logit $z = wx+b$</strong> changes when <strong className="text-white">Weight $w$</strong> changes ($= x$).</div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-5">
        <strong className="text-white">What is Backpropagation?</strong> Backpropagation is literally just the <strong className="text-cyan-300">Chain Rule applied from right to left</strong> (from the output Loss back to the early layers), caching intermediate derivatives so the GPU never recalculates the same derivative twice!
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-pink-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      6. Higher-Order Derivatives: Jacobians and Hessians
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        As you read advanced AI papers, you will encounter two matrix generalizations of partial derivatives:
      </div>
      <div className="grid gap-5 md:grid-cols-2">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-pink-500/25 bg-pink-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-2 font-bold text-pink-300">1. The Jacobian Matrix ($\mathbf{J}$) — 1st Order</div>
          <div className="mb-4 text-sm text-slate-300">
            When a function takes a vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and outputs a <strong className="text-white">vector</strong> $\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^m$ (like a Softmax layer), the Jacobian is the $(m \times n)$ grid of all first-order partial derivatives:
          </div>
          <div className="rounded-lg bg-slate-950 p-4 text-center text-sm">
            <div>$$J_{i,j} = \dfrac{\partial y_i}{\partial x_j}$$</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-cyan-500/25 bg-cyan-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-2 font-bold text-cyan-300">2. The Hessian Matrix ($\mathbf{H}$) — 2nd Order</div>
          <div className="mb-4 text-sm text-slate-300">

          </div>

              <span className="text-amber-400 transition group-open:rotate-180">▼</span>

              <strong className="mb-1 block text-rose-400">✕ Incorrect.</strong>

        <div className="flex items-start gap-3">
          <span className="mt-0.5 font-bold text-emerald-400">✓</span>
          <span>A derivative $\dfrac{df}{dx}$ measures the instantaneous rate of change (slope) of an output with respect to a small nudge in its input.</span>
        </div>
        <div className="flex items-start gap-3">
          <span className="mt-0.5 font-bold text-emerald-400">✓</span>
          <span>A partial derivative $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w_i}$ isolates the sensitivity of one specific weight $w_i$ by treating all other parameters in the model as frozen constants.</span>
        </div>
        <div className="flex items-start gap-3">
          <span className="mt-0.5 font-bold text-emerald-400">✓</span>
          <span>The Gradient Vector $\nabla L$ collects all partial derivatives into a single vector that points in the direction of steepest error increase; stepping in $-\nabla L$ minimizes error fastest.</span>
        </div>
        <div className="flex items-start gap-3">
          <span className="mt-0.5 font-bold text-emerald-400">✓</span>
          <span>The Chain Rule ($\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a} \dfrac{\partial a}{\partial z} \dfrac{\partial z}{\partial w}$) multiplies local derivatives across nested layers and is the exact mathematical engine of Backpropagation.</span>
        </div>
        <div className="flex items-start gap-3">
          <span className="mt-0.5 font-bold text-emerald-400">✓</span>
          <span>Activation functions with tiny derivatives (like Sigmoid, where $\sigma^\prime(z) \le 0.25$) cause vanishing gradients when multiplied across many layers, which is why modern networks prefer ReLU or GELU.</span>
        </div>
        <div className="flex items-start gap-3">
          <span className="mt-0.5 font-bold text-emerald-400">✓</span>
          <span>The Jacobian matrix stores first-order partial derivatives of vector-to-vector functions, while the Hessian matrix stores second-order derivatives that describe loss surface curvature.</span>
        </div>

    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-rose-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      Common Mistakes
    </h2>
    <div className="rounded-2xl border border-rose-500/25 bg-rose-950/[0.15] p-6 sm:p-8">
      <div className="space-y-6">
        <div>
          <div className="mb-1.5 font-bold text-rose-400">
            ✕ Differentiating with respect to the input data x instead of the parameters w.
          </div>
          <div className="text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            During model training, your dataset features $\mathbf{x}$ and labels $y$ are fixed constants. Always take partial derivatives with respect to the learnable weights $\mathbf{w}$ and biases $\mathbf{b}$.
          </div>
        </div>
        <div>
          <div className="mb-1.5 font-bold text-rose-400">
            ✕ Adding the gradient instead of subtracting it during weight updates.
          </div>
          <div className="text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            Because the gradient $\nabla L$ points uphill toward maximum error, updating weights via $\mathbf{w} + \alpha \nabla L$ performs Gradient Ascent and causes the model&apos;s loss to explode. Always subtract: $\mathbf{w} - \alpha \nabla L$.
          </div>
        </div>
        <div>
          <div className="mb-1.5 font-bold text-rose-400">
            ✕ Confusing a zero gradient (dL/dw = 0) with a guaranteed global minimum.
          </div>
          <div className="text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            A slope of zero simply means the surface is locally flat. In high-dimensional neural networks, $\nabla L = \mathbf{0}$ can be a local minimum, a maximum peak, or most commonly a saddle point.
          </div>
        </div>
        <div>
          <div className="mb-1.5 font-bold text-rose-400">
            ✕ Using finite differences (numerical derivatives) to train neural networks.
          </div>
          <div className="text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            Computing $\dfrac{L(w+\epsilon) - L(w-\epsilon)}{2\epsilon}$ requires two full forward passes per parameter—which would take billions of forward passes for one step in an LLM. Only use numerical gradients for unit-testing, and use analytical Backpropagation for training.
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>
