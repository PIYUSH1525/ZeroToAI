  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-blue-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      1. Beginner: The Blindfolded Hiker (The Core Intuition)
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        Imagine you are standing on a foggy mountain, blindfolded, and your goal is to reach the lowest valley. You cannot see the whole landscape. All you can do is feel the slope of the ground under your feet. If the ground tilts downward to your right, you take a small step to the right. Then you feel the slope again and repeat. Step by step, you walk downhill until the ground feels flat. That is gradient descent.
      </div>
      <div className="grid gap-5 md:grid-cols-3">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-blue-500/25 bg-blue-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-1 flex items-center justify-between">
            <span className="font-bold text-blue-300">The Mountain</span>
            <span className="rounded bg-blue-950/80 px-2 py-0.5 font-mono text-xs text-blue-300">Loss Function</span>
          </div>
          <div className="mt-2 space-y-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            <div><strong className="text-white">Meaning:</strong> How wrong the model is for the current parameter values. High = wrong, low = good.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-violet-500/25 bg-violet-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-1 flex items-center justify-between">
            <span className="font-bold text-violet-300">Your Position</span>
            <span className="rounded bg-violet-950/80 px-2 py-0.5 font-mono text-xs text-violet-300">Parameters</span>
          </div>
          <div className="mt-2 space-y-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            <div><strong className="text-white">Meaning:</strong> The weights and biases the model can change. Moving means changing these numbers.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/25 bg-emerald-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-1 flex items-center justify-between">
            <span className="font-bold text-emerald-300">The Slope</span>
            <span className="rounded bg-emerald-950/80 px-2 py-0.5 font-mono text-xs text-emerald-300">Gradient</span>
          </div>
          <div className="mt-2 space-y-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            <div><strong className="text-white">Meaning:</strong> Tells you which direction is uphill and how steep it is, so you can walk the other way.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-5">
        <div className="mb-2 font-bold text-white">Why not just solve it with a formula?</div>
        <div className="text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
          For very simple models there is a direct formula for the best parameters. But a modern neural network has millions or billions of parameters and a bumpy, complicated loss surface. No direct formula exists, so we use a patient step-by-step search instead. Gradient descent is that search, and it powers almost every model you will meet in this course.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-violet-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      2. Beginner: The Loss Landscape and What a Gradient Really Is
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        A <strong className="text-white">loss function</strong> $J(w)$ turns the model&apos;s parameters into a single number that measures its error. For example, in linear regression we often use the average squared error between predictions and true values. Plotting $J(w)$ against $w$ gives a curve (for one parameter) or a surface (for many parameters). We want the lowest point.
      </div>
      <div>
        The <strong className="text-white">gradient</strong> is the derivative of the loss with respect to the parameters. With one parameter it is just the slope of the curve at the current point:
      </div>
      <div className="grid gap-4 md:grid-cols-3">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-rose-500/25 bg-rose-500/[0.04] p-5">
          <div className="font-bold text-rose-300">Slope is positive</div>
          <div className="mt-2 text-sm text-slate-300">The loss rises as $w$ increases, so the way down is to <strong className="text-white">decrease</strong> $w$.</div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/25 bg-emerald-500/[0.04] p-5">
          <div className="font-bold text-emerald-300">Slope is negative</div>
          <div className="mt-2 text-sm text-slate-300">The loss falls as $w$ increases, so the way down is to <strong className="text-white">increase</strong> $w$.</div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-cyan-500/25 bg-cyan-500/[0.04] p-5">
          <div className="font-bold text-cyan-300">Slope is zero</div>
          <div className="mt-2 text-sm text-slate-300">The ground is flat. We have reached a minimum (or occasionally a flat spot).</div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div>
        With many parameters, the gradient $\nabla J$ is a list of slopes, one per parameter. Together they point in the direction of the <em>steepest uphill</em>. So gradient <em>descent</em> always walks in the opposite direction.
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-emerald-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      3. Medium: The Update Rule and a Worked Example
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        Every step of gradient descent uses one simple rule. Subtract the gradient, scaled by a small number called the <strong className="text-white">learning rate</strong> $\alpha$:
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-[#060913] p-5 text-center text-white">
        $$w_{new} = w_{old} - \alpha \cdot \frac{\partial J}{\partial w}$$
      </div>
      <div>
        Let us run it by hand on a tiny loss function, $J(w) = (w - 4)^2$. Its lowest point is clearly at $w = 4$, and its gradient is $\frac{\partial J}{\partial w} = 2(w - 4)$. Start at $w = 0$ with $\alpha = 0.1$:
      </div>
      <div className="overflow-x-auto rounded-2xl border border-white/10">
        <table className="w-full text-left text-sm">
          <thead className="bg-white/[0.04] text-xs uppercase tracking-wider text-slate-400">
            <tr>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">Step</th>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">Current $w$</th>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">Gradient $2(w-4)$</th>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">Loss $J(w)$</th>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">New $w$</th>
            </tr>
          </thead>
          <tbody className="divide-y divide-white/5 text-slate-300">
            <tr><td className="px-5 py-3">0</td><td className="px-5 py-3">0.0000</td><td className="px-5 py-3">-8.000</td><td className="px-5 py-3">16.000</td><td className="px-5 py-3 text-emerald-300">0.8000</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-5 py-3">1</td><td className="px-5 py-3">0.8000</td><td className="px-5 py-3">-6.400</td><td className="px-5 py-3">10.240</td><td className="px-5 py-3 text-emerald-300">1.4400</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-5 py-3">2</td><td className="px-5 py-3">1.4400</td><td className="px-5 py-3">-5.120</td><td className="px-5 py-3">6.554</td><td className="px-5 py-3 text-emerald-300">1.9520</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-5 py-3">3</td><td className="px-5 py-3">1.9520</td><td className="px-5 py-3">-4.096</td><td className="px-5 py-3">4.194</td><td className="px-5 py-3 text-emerald-300">2.3616</td></tr>
          </tbody>
        </table>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/20 bg-emerald-500/[0.05] p-5 text-sm leading-relaxed">
        <strong className="text-emerald-300">Notice two things.</strong> The loss shrinks every step, and the steps get smaller as we approach the minimum, because the slope flattens out. For this function the remaining distance to $w = 4$ is multiplied by $(1 - 2\alpha) = 0.8$ at every step, so we get closer and closer without overshooting.
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-orange-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      4. Medium: The Learning Rate Decides Everything
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        The learning rate $\alpha$ is the size of each step, and it is the single most important setting in gradient descent. On the same function $J(w) = (w-4)^2$ the error is multiplied by $(1 - 2\alpha)$ per step, which makes the three classic cases easy to see:
      </div>
      <div className="grid gap-5 md:grid-cols-3">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-blue-500/25 bg-blue-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-1 flex items-center justify-between">
            <span className="font-bold text-blue-300">Too Small</span>
            <span className="rounded bg-blue-950/80 px-2 py-0.5 font-mono text-xs text-blue-300">$\alpha = 0.01$</span>
          </div>
          <div className="mt-2 space-y-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            <div><strong className="text-white">Factor per step:</strong> $0.98$</div>
            <div><strong className="text-white">Behavior:</strong> Safe but painfully slow. Thousands of steps may be needed.</div>
            <div><strong className="text-white">Risk:</strong> Wastes compute and can stall on flat regions.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/25 bg-emerald-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-1 flex items-center justify-between">
            <span className="font-bold text-emerald-300">Just Right</span>
            <span className="rounded bg-emerald-950/80 px-2 py-0.5 font-mono text-xs text-emerald-300">$\alpha = 0.1$</span>
          </div>
          <div className="mt-2 space-y-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            <div><strong className="text-white">Factor per step:</strong> $0.80$</div>
            <div><strong className="text-white">Behavior:</strong> Smooth, steady approach to the minimum.</div>
            <div><strong className="text-white">Risk:</strong> Low. This is the goal.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-rose-500/25 bg-rose-500/[0.04] p-6">
          <div className="mb-1 flex items-center justify-between">
            <span className="font-bold text-rose-300">Too Large</span>
            <span className="rounded bg-rose-950/80 px-2 py-0.5 font-mono text-xs text-rose-300">$\alpha = 1.1$</span>
          </div>
          <div className="mt-2 space-y-2 text-xs leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
            <div><strong className="text-white">Factor per step:</strong> $-1.2$ (size grows)</div>
            <div><strong className="text-white">Behavior:</strong> Each step overshoots the valley and lands higher up the other side.</div>
            <div><strong className="text-white">Risk:</strong> The loss explodes. This is called divergence.</div>
          </div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-amber-500/25 bg-amber-500/[0.05] p-5 text-sm leading-relaxed">
        <strong className="text-amber-300">Practical tip:</strong> If your loss goes up, becomes <code>nan</code>, or jumps around wildly, lower the learning rate first. If the loss falls but extremely slowly, try raising it a little. Later you will learn learning-rate schedules that change $\alpha$ during training.
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-cyan-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">
      5. Advanced: Batch, Stochastic and Mini-Batch Gradient Descent
    </h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>
        Real datasets can have millions of rows. The loss gradient is an average over the training examples, so there is a choice: how many examples should we use to estimate the gradient before taking each step?
      </div>
      <div className="overflow-x-auto rounded-2xl border border-white/10">
        <table className="w-full text-left text-sm">
          <thead className="bg-white/[0.04] text-xs uppercase tracking-wider text-slate-400">
            <tr>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">Variant</th>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">Examples per step</th>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">Strength</th>
              <th className="px-5 py-3">Weakness</th>
            </tr>
          </thead>
          <tbody className="divide-y divide-white/5 text-slate-300">
            <tr><td className="px-5 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Batch</td><td className="px-5 py-3">All of them</td><td className="px-5 py-3">Accurate, smooth path</td><td className="px-5 py-3">Slow and memory-heavy on big data</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-5 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Stochastic (SGD)</td><td className="px-5 py-3">Exactly 1</td><td className="px-5 py-3">Very fast updates</td><td className="px-5 py-3">Noisy, zig-zag path</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-5 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Mini-batch</td><td className="px-5 py-3">A small group, such as 32 or 256</td><td className="px-5 py-3">Good balance, fits GPUs well</td><td className="px-5 py-3">Needs a batch size choice</td></tr>
          </tbody>
        </table>
      </div>
      <div>
        Almost all deep learning uses <strong className="text-white">mini-batch</strong> gradient descent. The small amount of noise it adds is usually helpful, because it can shake the parameters out of shallow traps. Modern optimizers such as momentum, RMSProp and Adam are improved versions of this same idea: they still follow the gradient, but they adapt the step size and direction using the history of past gradients.
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-5">
        <div className="mb-2 font-bold text-white">Local minima, saddle points and plateaus</div>
        <div className="text-sm leading-relaxed text-slate-300">
          The bowl-shaped loss in our example has one minimum, so gradient descent always finds it. Neural network losses are bumpier. Gradient descent can slow down on flat plateaus and saddle points (flat in one direction, curved in another). In practice, for large networks, the noise of mini-batches and smarter optimizers make this much less of a problem than it first sounds.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>
  </section>
