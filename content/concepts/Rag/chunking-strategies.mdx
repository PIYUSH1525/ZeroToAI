    <div className="rounded-2xl border border-cyan-500/20 bg-cyan-500/[0.05] p-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300"><strong className="text-cyan-400">The Core Idea:</strong> A document is too long to embed, search and place in a prompt as one piece, so it is cut into <strong className="text-white">chunks</strong>. A chunk is the unit that RAG indexes, retrieves and shows to the model. Cut too small and the facts lose their context. Cut too large and each chunk mixes many topics and matches nothing precisely. Good chunking is one of the biggest levers on RAG quality.</div>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-blue-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">1. Beginner: Why Documents Must Be Chunked</h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>Imagine searching a 300-page manual by asking a librarian for the whole book every time. You would rather ask for the exact page. Chunking turns a long document into page-sized pieces so retrieval can return just the part that answers the question.</div>
      <div className="grid gap-5 md:grid-cols-3">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-blue-500/25 bg-blue-500/[0.04] p-5">
          <div className="mb-2 font-semibold text-blue-300">Embedding limits</div>
          <div className="text-sm leading-7">Embedding models accept only a limited amount of text, and one vector for a whole book blurs many topics into a single average meaning.</div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-violet-500/25 bg-violet-500/[0.04] p-5">
          <div className="mb-2 font-semibold text-violet-300">Precision</div>
          <div className="text-sm leading-7">A small focused chunk matches a specific question strongly. A huge chunk matches weakly because most of it is unrelated.</div>
        </div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/25 bg-emerald-500/[0.04] p-5">
          <div className="mb-2 font-semibold text-emerald-300">Prompt budget</div>
          <div className="text-sm leading-7">The prompt can hold only a few passages. Short relevant chunks fit many sources; long ones crowd each other out.</div>
        </div>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-blue-500/20 bg-blue-500/[0.05] p-5"><strong className="text-blue-300">Key point:</strong> the chunk is the unit of retrieval. Whatever you cut at indexing time is exactly what the retriever can find and the model can read later.</div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-violet-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">2. Beginner: Fixed-Size Chunking and Overlap</h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>The simplest method cuts the text every N tokens or words, ignoring its meaning. It is fast, predictable and easy to tune. Its weakness is that a cut can land in the middle of a fact, leaving half in one chunk and half in the next.</div>
      <div><strong className="text-white">Overlap</strong> repeats the last few words of one chunk at the start of the next, so a fact that crosses a boundary still appears whole in at least one chunk. With text length $L$, chunk size $C$ and overlap $O$ (all in the same unit), the number of chunks is about:</div>
      <div className="overflow-x-auto rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-5 text-center">$$\left\lceil \dfrac{L - O}{C - O} \right\rceil$$</div>
      <div>For example, $L = 1000$, $C = 200$ and $O = 50$ gives $\lceil 950 / 150 \rceil = 7$ chunks. Overlap costs extra storage and embedding work, so teams often start with a modest overlap, commonly around 10 to 20 percent of the chunk size, and tune it.</div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-emerald-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">3. Intermediate: Sentence, Paragraph and Recursive Chunking</h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>Structure-aware methods cut along natural boundaries instead of a fixed count, so chunks read like complete thoughts.</div>
      <div className="overflow-x-auto rounded-xl border border-white/10">
        <table className="w-full text-left text-sm">
          <thead className="bg-white/[0.04] text-slate-200">
            <tr><th className="px-4 py-3">Strategy</th><th className="px-4 py-3">How it cuts</th><th className="px-4 py-3">Strength</th><th className="px-4 py-3">Weakness</th></tr>
          </thead>
          <tbody className="divide-y divide-white/10">
            <tr><td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Sentence</td><td className="px-4 py-3">Groups a few whole sentences</td><td className="px-4 py-3">Never splits a sentence</td><td className="px-4 py-3">Sizes vary a lot</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Paragraph</td><td className="px-4 py-3">One chunk per paragraph</td><td className="px-4 py-3">Keeps a full idea together</td><td className="px-4 py-3">Paragraphs can be tiny or huge</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Recursive</td><td className="px-4 py-3">Tries big separators first, then smaller ones until each piece fits</td><td className="px-4 py-3">Good default; respects structure and size limit</td><td className="px-4 py-3">Needs sensible separators</td></tr>
          </tbody>
        </table>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-emerald-500/20 bg-emerald-500/[0.05] p-5"><strong className="text-emerald-300">How recursive chunking works:</strong> split on paragraph breaks first. Any piece still too long is split on line breaks, then sentences, then words. Each piece is kept as large as the size limit allows, so structure is preserved wherever possible.</div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-orange-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">4. Intermediate: Semantic Chunking</h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div><strong className="text-white">Semantic chunking</strong> starts a new chunk when the topic changes. The text is split into sentences, each is embedded, and the similarity between neighboring sentences is measured. Where similarity drops below a threshold, a boundary is placed.</div>
      <div className="space-y-3">
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-4"><strong className="text-orange-300">Step 1.</strong> Split the document into sentences.</div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-4"><strong className="text-orange-300">Step 2.</strong> Embed every sentence.</div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-4"><strong className="text-orange-300">Step 3.</strong> Compare each sentence with the next one.</div>
        <div className="rounded-xl border border-white/10 bg-white/[0.03] p-4"><strong className="text-orange-300">Step 4.</strong> Cut where the similarity falls sharply, then merge small leftovers.</div>
      </div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-orange-500/20 bg-orange-500/[0.05] p-5"><strong className="text-orange-300">Trade-off:</strong> semantic chunks follow meaning, but they need an embedding call for every sentence at indexing time and a threshold that must be tuned. Many teams find recursive chunking nearly as good and far cheaper, so measure before you commit.</div>
    </div>
  </section>

  <section>
    <h2 className="mb-6 border-l-4 border-cyan-500 pl-4 text-2xl font-bold tracking-tight text-white sm:text-3xl">5. Advanced: Choosing a Chunk Size by Measuring</h2>
    <div className="space-y-6 text-[16px] leading-8 text-slate-300">
      <div>There is no universal best size. Small chunks give precise matches but may lack context. Large chunks carry context but dilute the match and use more of the prompt budget.</div>
      <div className="overflow-x-auto rounded-xl border border-white/10">
        <table className="w-full text-left text-sm">
          <thead className="bg-white/[0.04] text-slate-200">
            <tr><th className="px-4 py-3">Aspect</th><th className="px-4 py-3">Small chunks</th><th className="px-4 py-3">Large chunks</th></tr>
          </thead>
          <tbody className="divide-y divide-white/10">
            <tr><td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Match precision</td><td className="px-4 py-3 text-emerald-300">High</td><td className="px-4 py-3 text-rose-300">Lower</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Context inside the chunk</td><td className="px-4 py-3 text-rose-300">Often too little</td><td className="px-4 py-3 text-emerald-300">Usually enough</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Prompt space used</td><td className="px-4 py-3 text-emerald-300">Little each</td><td className="px-4 py-3 text-rose-300">Much each</td></tr>
            <tr><td className="px-4 py-3 font-semibold text-white">Index size</td><td className="px-4 py-3 text-rose-300">More vectors</td><td className="px-4 py-3 text-emerald-300">Fewer vectors</td></tr>
          </tbody>
        </table>
      </div>
      <div>A common starting point is a few hundred tokens per chunk with light overlap, but treat that as a hypothesis. Build a small set of real questions with known answer locations, try several sizes, and compare how often the right chunk appears in the top results. The number that wins on your data is the right one.</div>
      <div className="rounded-xl border border-cyan-500/20 bg-cyan-500/[0.05] p-5"><strong className="text-cyan-300">Always keep metadata:</strong> store the source, section and position of every chunk. It makes citations possible, lets you fetch neighboring chunks, and prepares the ground for the parent and child chunks in the next lesson.</div>
    </div>
  </section>
